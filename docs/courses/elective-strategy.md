# Elective Strategy: Getting the Most Out of Your Free Choices

Your electives are the most underutilized part of your CS degree. Most students pick whatever fits their schedule or sounds easy. That's fine, but with a little strategy, you can use your electives to build skills that genuinely differentiate you in the job market, or at least protect your GPA during brutal semesters.

This guide is for students who want to be intentional about their choices.

---

## First: Understanding What You're Actually Choosing

### Required Electives vs Free Electives

In the UofA CS Major and Honors programs, not all electives are equal. You'll have:

- **CMPUT electives:** Upper-year CMPUT courses beyond your required courses. These count toward your CS credit requirements and let you go deeper in specific areas.
- **Science electives:** Non-CMPUT courses from science disciplines (MATH, STAT, PHYS, CHEM, BIOL, etc.). CS has specific requirements here; check the current calendar.
- **Non-science electives:** Courses from Arts, Business, Fine Arts, etc. Check the BSc rules for how many non-science units you can count.
- **Free electives:** Whatever fills the remaining units of the 120-unit BSc after all other requirements, including the [common course requirements](https://calendar.ualberta.ca/preview_program.php?catoid=69&poid=110969) (6 units of ENGL/WRS and a 3-unit Indigenous course).

Some courses people treat as electives are actually requirements. An intro stats course (STAT 151, 235, or 265) is required in every CS path, and a second stats course (STAT 252 or 266) is required in Honors and in the AI and Software Practice Options ([calendar](https://calendar.ualberta.ca/preview_program.php?catoid=69&poid=110935)). Don't spend elective planning on those; they're already in your plan.

The exact counts change slightly with calendar updates, so always verify in the current UofA Academic Calendar and confirm on BearTracks before registering.

### How to Check What Counts in BearTracks

1. Log in to BearTracks and go to your **Academic Advisement Report** (AAR).
2. The AAR maps every course you've taken or plan to take against your degree requirements.
3. When you add a course to your "what-if" or planned courses, it will show you which requirement bucket it fills.
4. If you're unsure whether a specific course counts as a science elective or free elective, add it to your planner and check the AAR, or email your departmental advisor. Don't guess.

This takes ten minutes and will save you from the nightmare of realizing in your final semester that a course didn't count where you thought it did.

## GPA-Boosting Electives: Honest Talk

Let's be real: sometimes you're taking a brutal semester (say, 204 + 229 + 291) and you need an elective that won't destroy you. There's nothing wrong with this. Sustainable GPA management is part of getting through the degree.

The truth, though, is that "easy" is deeply personal:

- **PHIL courses** (especially intro logic courses like PHIL 120, Symbolic Logic I, and PHIL 125, Practical Logic) are commonly considered lighter by CS students. PHIL 120's sentential and predicate logic overlaps with CMPUT 272, so it doesn't feel entirely foreign.
- **ENGL courses** (intro literature, writing courses) are often mentioned as less demanding for quantitatively-oriented students, but if you hate writing essays, these will feel hard regardless.
- **Some AREC, ANTHR, SOC intro courses** have reputations for lighter workloads, but this varies enormously by instructor and year.
- **PEDS courses** (physical education) count as electives and are often low-stress, useful if you want credit hours without academic burden.

Ask upper-year students in your circle what worked for them. RateMyProfessors is imperfect but has signal. The UofA subreddit and CS Discord servers have threads on this.

**Warning:** Don't load up on "easy" electives at the expense of actually useful ones. You have limited elective slots. Spend them wisely.

## Actually Useful Electives for CS Careers

These are courses outside CMPUT that will meaningfully improve your career prospects or technical depth. Sorted by career relevance.

### Statistics and Data

Your CS program already includes intro stats (STAT 151, 235, or 265) and, in Honors and the Options, a second stats course (STAT 252 or 266). Those are requirements, not electives. The elective decision is mostly about **which** of them you take:

**STAT 265: Probability and Statistics I** (instead of STAT 151)
Sample spaces, conditional probability, Bayes' theorem, random variables, expectation, multivariate distributions ([STAT catalogue](https://apps.ualberta.ca/catalogue/course/stat)). It's the more rigorous probability route, it satisfies the intro stats requirement, and it's one of the courses that unlocks CMPUT 365 (Introduction to Reinforcement Learning). It has a corequisite of MATH 209, 214, or 217, so plan for that.

**STAT 252: Introduction to Applied Statistics II** or **STAT 266: Probability and Statistics II**
STAT 252 covers regression, analysis of variance, and data analysis methods; 266 continues the 265 track. If your path requires the second course, pick the one that fits the first.

**Why it matters in industry:** A/B testing is everywhere in product companies. Being able to say "this result is statistically significant at the 95% confidence level and here's why" is not a skill most engineers have. It's immediately useful and differentiating.

### Mathematics

All titles and prerequisites below are from the [MATH catalogue](https://apps.ualberta.ca/catalogue/course/math).

**MATH 214: Calculus III**
Series and Taylor series, partial differentiation, multiple integration. Useful for physics simulations, game engines, and ML (gradient descent is calculus). It's also a corequisite option for STAT 265 and a prerequisite option for CMPUT 340.

**MATH 225: Linear Algebra II**
The follow-up to MATH 125: vector spaces, inner product spaces, Gram-Schmidt, QR factorization and least squares, diagonalization, quadratic forms. Directly useful for ML and graphics, and it's one of the prerequisite options for CMPUT 340 (Introduction to Numerical Methods).

**MATH 256: Elementary Number Theory**
Divisibility, primes, congruences, quadratic residues. Relevant for cryptography (pairs well with CMPUT 331, Computational Cryptography) and competitive programming. Requires MATH 125 or 127.

**MATH 322: Graph Theory**
Reinforces the graph material from CMPUT 204 from a proof-based angle. Requires linear algebra plus any 200-level MATH course.

**MATH 381: Numerical Methods**
Interpolation, splines, least squares, solving linear and non-linear systems. Note that you **can't get credit for MATH 381 if you have credit for CMPUT 340** (or take them in the same term), so pick one.

### Linguistics

**LING 101: Introduction to Linguistic Analysis**
If you have any interest in NLP (natural language processing), computational linguistics, or working on language models, this is valuable background. It covers phonetics, phonology, morphology, syntax, and semantics, and it's the prerequisite for 200-level LING courses like LING 204 (Syntax of the World's Languages). Also: a genuinely interesting course that changes how you think about language.

**Industry relevance for NLP/ML:** High. Understanding linguistic structure makes you better at NLP tasks and helps you communicate with linguists on research teams.

### Psychology

**PSYCH 104: Basic Psychological Processes**
Useful if you're interested in HCI (human-computer interaction), UX research, product design, or anything user-facing. Understanding cognitive load, attention, memory limitations, and decision-making biases makes you a better product engineer. The catalogue tags PSYCH 104 as a Faculty of Science course. Many UX researchers have psychology backgrounds; knowing the vocabulary helps you collaborate with them.

### Philosophy

**PHIL 120: Symbolic Logic I / PHIL 125: Practical Logic**
PHIL 120 is sentential logic and an introduction to predicate logic; PHIL 125 is everyday reasoning, informal fallacies, and elementary statistical reasoning ([PHIL catalogue](https://apps.ualberta.ca/catalogue/course/phil)). PHIL 120 will feel familiar after CMPUT 272. Check your degree audit for how PHIL counts toward your non-science units.

**PHIL 220: Symbolic Logic II**
Goes deeper: predicate logic with identity, natural deduction, induction, elementary modal logic, formal axiomatic systems. Relevant if you're interested in programming language theory, formal verification, or type systems. Requires PHIL 120 (or department consent).

### Business

**SMO / BUS courses: Project Management, Organizational Behavior, Intro Business**
Underrated by CS students who want to write code forever. Reality: within a few years of graduation, you'll be navigating organizations, advocating for projects, and working with non-technical stakeholders. Some business vocabulary goes a long way. A business minor is achievable and opens doors to consulting, product management, and startup roles that pure CS students sometimes miss out on.

## Minor Strategies

Minors are optional but can add real value. Here are the most useful ones for CS students:

### Statistics Minor

Excellent for ML, data science, and any role touching data. If you're planning an ML career, this is one of the best signals you can put on a resume; it shows mathematical rigor, not just "I did a Kaggle competition."

Some of the STAT courses your CS program already requires may overlap with a minor; check the UofA calendar for the current minor requirements and how double-counting works.

### Mathematics Minor

Good for ML, theoretical CS, and research paths. Also looks strong to employers who care about analytical rigor. Takes commitment but the math is useful.

### Business Minor (through the Alberta School of Business)

Opens doors to PM roles, consulting, and startup work. Business minors have to navigate cross-registration between faculties, so check requirements early; some Business courses are restricted to Business students. Talk to an advisor in your first or second year if you're considering this.

### Linguistics Minor

Genuinely useful for NLP careers. Not commonly taken by CS students, which makes it stand out. Works well if you're also doing ML courses.

## Timing Strategy: When to Take What

This is the part most guides skip. Elective timing matters.

**Heavy CS semesters** (typically when you're taking 204, 379, 291, or multiple upper-year CMPUT courses): Take easier electives. This is your "protect your GPA and sanity" semester. A light humanities course or an intro STAT course is fine here.

**Lighter CS semesters** (first semester of a year, or when you've knocked out the major requirements): Take substantive electives: a full STAT course, a MATH course, something that adds real depth.

**Final year:** Be careful. Many students underestimate their capstone or final project load. Don't stack challenging electives in the same semester as CMPUT 401.

**Rule of thumb:** One "challenging and useful" elective per semester is sustainable. Two is pushing it. Don't take three upper-level non-CS courses while also taking upper-level CMPUT courses.

## A Note for International Students

If English is not your first language, you may have ENGL course requirements as part of your admission conditions or program requirements. Check with your academic advisor. These aren't optional in your case; they're mandated, and trying to waive them is usually more work than just taking them. The good news: many international students find that improving their written English directly improves their technical communication skills, which matters in interviews and on the job.

---

## The Honest Summary

- Verify everything on BearTracks; don't assume a course counts where you think it does.
- Your intro and second stats courses are requirements, not electives; if you like math, choosing STAT 265/266 over 151/252 is a cheap upgrade.
- Use easy electives strategically during heavy semesters, not as a default approach.
- If you have a career direction (ML, security, product), pick electives that reinforce it.
- Minors are worth considering if you start planning early (first or second year).
- Ask upper-year students and recent grads what they wished they'd taken. They'll give you better advice than any official guide will.
